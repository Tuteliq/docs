# SITO research paper

SITO is a test set, and a research paper about it, that measures two ways a safety system can fail: **missing harm**, and **flagging harmless messages**. The paper used it to test six of Tuteliq's detections in English, Ukrainian and mixed Ukrainian. This page explains what was tested and what was found, including where Tuteliq fell short. It is a summary. If anything here differs from the paper, the paper is correct.

The name stands for "Synthetic Independent Text-safety benchmark with Overflagging tests". *Sito* is Ukrainian for "sieve".

## Who ran it and who paid

- **Author:** Andrii Tuzhykov, AI/ML consultant and PhD researcher at the State Institution South Ukrainian National Pedagogical University named after K. D. Ushynsky.
- **Commissioned and funded by:** ImproveIT Solutions.

<Warning>
**Tuteliq arranged this study.** The paper's competing-interests statement says that ImproveIT Solutions was engaged for the study by Tuteliq AB, the company behind Tuteliq. Tuteliq gave free access to its production API, an API key and its evaluation harness (the tool Tuteliq uses to run outside evaluators' test sets through its API). The paper states that Tuteliq saw the manuscript only to correct factual errors and had no editorial control, that the design, analysis and conclusions are the author's, and that the author received no payment from Tuteliq. This is the sense in which the paper calls the study independent.
</Warning>

### Why the study was done

The paper notes that independent evidence on how often commercial safety systems miss harm or flag harmless text is scarce, especially outside English. It also notes that Tuteliq's published model card describes a different model version, says none of its figures is a third-party evaluation, and leaves the false-positive rate on harmless text unquantified.

## What was tested

The paper tested the Tuteliq release `tuteliq-detect-v5.20261011`, not the separately published v6 model. Its results hold for that release, not for v6 or later releases. All three main test runs were made on October 12, 2026.

Six detections were tested:

- **Authorised push-payment fraud** (app fraud): scams where the victim is persuaded to send the money themselves.
- **Money-mule recruitment:** recruiting people to move money through their own accounts.
- **Social engineering:** manipulating someone into giving up information or access.
- **Romance scams**
- **Gambling harm**
- **Bullying**

### The test cases

SITO has **2,332 synthetic cases**: 1,179 harmful and 1,153 harmless. Of these, 1,410 are in English and 922 are in Ukrainian or mixed language. The mixed-language cases cover:

- **Surzhyk:** a mixed variety of Ukrainian and Russian.
- **Ukrainian–Russian** and **Ukrainian–English** switching within a conversation.
- **Ukrainian written in the Latin alphabet.**

All cases were written by AI models, contain no personal data, and were written for this study. None comes from Tuteliq's data.

**What counts as harmful depends on who is speaking.** A message is harmful when it is the harmful act itself (the perpetrator), when a victim describes harm that is happening now, or when someone else reports harm that is happening now. A message is harmless when a victim describes something that is already over, when it warns or educates, or when it is ordinary communication.

**The harmless cases are deliberately hard.** Most share the topic and wording of the harmful ones, such as a bank's genuine warning, a real job offer, a friendly bet between adults, or banter between friends. Some are **minimal-edit contrasts**: copies of a harmful case with every harmful element removed, so that only small changes separate harmful from harmless.

**How the cases were made and checked.** Cases were generated by Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5 (1,191 cases) and Google Gemini 3.1 Pro (1,087 cases). A further 54 English cases came from Alibaba Qwen 3.6 Plus as a "held-out" check: a model family not otherwise used, to see whether results depend on which AI wrote the text. A fourth AI model, OpenAI GPT-5.4 mini, labelled every case without seeing the intended label, and the author reviewed disagreements and samples. The review removed 68 cases. On the final sets, the AI checker agrees with the labels on 96.7% of cases.

### How results were scored

A case counts as **flagged** when Tuteliq recommends an action on it, such as review by a moderator or blocking. When Tuteliq sees no risk, or only records the message without asking anyone to act, the case is not flagged. No language, age or conversation setting was sent with the cases in the main runs.

Two measures matter most:

- **Recall:** of the harmful cases, the share Tuteliq flagged.
- **False-positive rate:** of the harmless cases, the share Tuteliq wrongly flagged.

The paper gives a 95% range for each figure, shown in square brackets below. A wide range means a figure is less certain.

## Main results (English)

| Detection | Harmful cases flagged (recall) | Harmless cases wrongly flagged |
|---|---|---|
| App fraud | 84.9% [77.4, 90.2] | 0.0% [0.0, 3.2] |
| Money-mule recruitment | 94.1% [88.3, 97.1] | 1.7% [0.5, 6.1] |
| Social engineering | 92.5% [86.4, 96.0] | 4.3% [1.8, 9.6] |
| Romance scam | 85.5% [78.0, 90.7] | 0.0% [0.0, 3.2] |
| Gambling harm | 85.6% [78.1, 90.8] | 0.0% [0.0, 3.2] |
| Bullying | 89.1% [82.2, 93.5] | 1.7% [0.5, 6.1] |

Source: the paper's Table 3, English cases, run 1.

Across all six, English recall was 88.6% and the false-positive rate was 1.3%. The paper concludes that Tuteliq errs towards missing harm rather than flagging harmless text: about one English harmful case in nine was not flagged.

**Which harmless messages were flagged.** None of the 133 educational texts, 126 accounts of past incidents or 99 unrelated everyday messages was flagged. False positives came from minimal-edit contrasts (10 of 171, 5.8%) and, less often, same-topic legitimate messages (8 of 624, 1.3%).

### Ukrainian and mixed language

Ukrainian and mixed-language input was detected at least as often as English. Every Ukrainian and mixed group had recall at or above the English 88.6%:

| Language group | Cases | Recall | False-positive rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 1,410 | 88.6% | 1.3% |
| Ukrainian | 337 | 92.6% | 4.3% |
| Ukrainian (translated from English) | 238 | 94.1% | 0.8% |
| Surzhyk | 95 | 97.9% | 0.0% |
| Ukrainian–Russian | 84 | 95.2% | 0.0% |
| Ukrainian–English | 85 | 100.0% | 2.3% |
| Ukrainian in Latin alphabet | 83 | 97.6% | 0.0% |

Source: the paper's Table 5, all six detections, run 1. The paper gives 95% ranges for every row; the smaller groups have wide ranges.

On cases translated between English and Ukrainian, the two languages did not differ. The paper notes that Tuteliq's model card lists Ukrainian as a beta language and does not mention Surzhyk, and calls this a better result than the documentation promises.

## Weaknesses the paper found

- **Reports from bystanders were often missed.** When someone else reported harm that was happening now, recall was 63.6% (68 of 107). This compares with 95.1% (774 of 814) for the perpetrator's own messages and 89.9% (232 of 258) for victims describing ongoing harm. These reports were sent without a declared setting. Tuteliq offers settings for reports of harm, for example to a fraud desk or a moderator, which the paper did not test.
- **Subtle harm was missed more often** than obvious harm: 88.2% recall against 93.6%. Conversations were also missed more often than single messages (87.7% against 92.7%), and had a higher false-positive rate (3.1% against 1.0%).
- **The weakest types of harm** were those where the harmful act is not yet a request: love bombing (58.8%), rumours and relational bullying (66.7%), moving the conversation off the platform (70.4%) and loss of control over gambling (75.0%). The paper treats these as exploratory, with 21 to 37 cases each.
- **Gambling harm: the score was right, the action was not.** Tuteliq gives each message a risk score and, separately, a recommended action. In gambling harm, the risk score separated harmful from harmless cases perfectly, but the recommended action missed 14.4% of harmful cases. The paper says these misses lie in how the score is turned into an action, not in the score itself.
- **More false positives on original Ukrainian text:** 4.3% against 1.3% for English, though the ranges overlap. All 7 false positives on original Ukrainian text were minimal-edit contrasts. The paper says the cause more likely lies in those contrasts than in the language, but cannot tell for certain.
- **Results may be optimistic.** Recall was 93.6% on cases written by Claude, 89.5% on Gemini, and 67.9% on the 54 cases written by the held-out Qwen model (19 of 28 harmful cases). False-positive rates were 0.7%, 2.3% and 7.7% (2 of 26). The Qwen check is small and English-only, but the paper says the headline recall may be optimistic for human-written text.
- **The same message can get a different answer.** Across three identical runs, 74 of 2,332 cases (3.2%) got a different decision in at least one run. On English text, recall for a detection moved by at most 3.4 percentage points between runs. The paper warns that borderline messages can pass on one call and be flagged on the next.

## Setting the conversation type (exploratory)

Tuteliq lets a platform declare what kind of conversation it is sending, such as a direct message, a group chat or a helpline (`conversation_type`). The paper made one extra run with this setting filled in, on October 13, 2026, under a newer release (`tuteliq-detect-v5.20261012`).

- Overall recall rose from 90.5% to 94.3% and the false-positive rate from 1.6% to 2.4%. The paper attributes this rise to changes between the two releases, not to the setting, because the rise also appeared on cases that declared no setting.
- On **helpline** conversations, the setting lowered alerts: 7 harmful cases lost their flag and 1 gained one (recall 92.9% to 87.6%). 4 of the 7 were reports from bystanders. The paper says this matches Tuteliq's guidance, and suits a helpline with a counsellor in the conversation, but not a platform that relies on Tuteliq to escalate such reports.

Because the release changed at the same time as the setting, the paper reports this run descriptively, outside its main questions.

## What this means at real-world scale

On a test set that is about half harmful, a low false-positive rate looks very good. On a real platform, harmful messages are rare, so even a small false-positive rate can mean that many of the flags a moderator sees are wrong. The paper calculates that if 1 message in 100 were harmful:

- In the three detections with any false positives (social engineering, bullying and money-mule recruitment), between 18% and 36% of flags would be correct.
- In the other three, no harmless case was flagged, but with 115 to 118 harmless English cases per detection, the data cannot rule out a false-positive rate of 3.2%. At that rate, only one flag in five would be correct.

The paper notes that its harmless cases were built to resemble harm, so these figures are a pessimistic reading for ordinary traffic, and an optimistic one for platforms whose traffic is mostly about money, dating or games.

## Limits of the study

The paper lists these limits:

- **Synthetic data only.** All cases were written by AI models. A planned set of human-written cases was not produced, so the gap to human-written text could not be measured directly.
- **One human reviewer.** No second human annotator was available, so agreement between human reviewers could not be reported. The paper suggests adding a native Ukrainian speaker familiar with Surzhyk.
- **One system, no comparison.** Only Tuteliq was tested, on text only. The figures say nothing about other vendors, though the released sets can be run against any other system.
- **Bystander settings not tested.** Reports from bystanders were tested without the settings Tuteliq offers for reports of harm.
- **Ranges may be too narrow.** The 95% ranges treat cases as independent, but some are not (for example, each minimal-edit contrast is built from a harmful case).

## What the paper recommends to platforms

1. Measure the false-positive rate on a sample of your own traffic, because no test set can tell you precision at your own level of harm.
2. Check how your users' roles are handled, particularly bystander reports and disclosures, and choose a conversation setting deliberately.
3. Do not assume a single call is final, because some borderline decisions change between identical runs.
4. Record the system version with every decision, because new releases come out often.

## Data and code

The paper releases the test sets, the generation instructions, the raw generated text, the review decisions and the code on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Improve-IT-Solutions/SITO), under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence, with access granted on request.
